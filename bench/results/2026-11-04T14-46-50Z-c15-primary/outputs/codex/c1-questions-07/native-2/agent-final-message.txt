Inflation means prices are rising **across many goods and services**, not just that one item has become more expensive. It doesn’t require people to buy more stuff.

Think about eggs at the grocery store. If a disease reduces the number of hens, fewer eggs reach the shelves. Shoppers may buy the same number of eggs as before, but now there are fewer eggs available. Stores raise prices, and some shoppers may pay more while others switch to a different food. The number of eggs bought overall might stay the same even though the price rises.

Costs can push prices up too. If feed, fuel, wages, or packaging become more expensive, it costs more to produce and deliver eggs. A grocery store may raise its price to cover those costs. That can happen even if customers don’t buy any more eggs.

More generally, prices depend partly on **demand**—how much people want and can afford to buy—and **supply**—how much is available to buy. If supply falls while demand stays about the same, sellers can charge more. If demand rises relative to supply, prices can rise even if the amount sold doesn’t: sellers may raise prices until some buyers cut back, leaving roughly the same quantity purchased.

But a price rise for eggs alone is **not necessarily inflation**. It may just be a change in the price of eggs. Inflation is when prices rise broadly across the economy—for example, when groceries, rent, transport, and many other things become more expensive over time. A shortage or cost increase affecting many parts of the economy can contribute to inflation; a problem limited to one item usually just makes that item more expensive.
