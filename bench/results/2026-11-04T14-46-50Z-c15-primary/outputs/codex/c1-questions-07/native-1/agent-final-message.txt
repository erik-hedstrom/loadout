Imagine you bought the same groceries this week as last week, but your bill went up. That can happen because **inflation is about prices rising, not about people buying more**. The same amount of money buys less than before.

Suppose a grocery store usually gets 100 cartons of milk each week, and shoppers buy 100. If a cold snap cuts deliveries to 80 cartons, shoppers don’t need to buy more for milk to become scarce. The store may raise the price, and some shoppers may buy less or switch to another drink. The quantity sold could stay the same—or even fall—while the price rises.

Prices can also rise when it costs more to grow, make, or deliver something. If fuel, fertilizer, wages, or electricity become more expensive, farmers and shops may pass some of those costs on to customers. Shoppers can end up buying the same amount of groceries at higher prices, or cut back because their budget no longer stretches as far.

Economists call the balance between what people want to buy and what’s available **demand and supply**. Demand means how much people want to buy at different prices; supply means how much sellers can provide. A shortage means there’s less available than people want at the current price. Prices can rise when supply falls, when demand rises, or when costs make it harder to supply goods—even if the final quantity bought doesn’t increase.

One item getting more expensive isn’t necessarily inflation. A jump in the price of milk might reflect a problem specific to milk. **Inflation** means prices are rising broadly across many goods and services in the economy, such as food, rent, transport, and utilities. A price increase in one category can contribute to inflation, but it isn’t the same as economy-wide inflation.
